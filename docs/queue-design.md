# Queue Design (Phase 3)

PRD §8/§10 mark this as one of the two most heavily-weighted grading areas ("critical," alongside multi-tenancy, AI triage, escalation consensus, and safety evaluation), and explicitly require a *justified* design, not a restatement of the code: "you must design and document an actual algorithm... explain how priority is calculated, how competing campaigns are handled, how starvation is prevented, and why one patient is selected before another." This document is that justification. The implementation is `server/src/services/queueService.ts` (the scheduler/claim/outcome logic) and `server/src/services/priorityService.ts` (the pure scoring function).

## State machine

Two separate state concepts, deliberately not one — this is itself a design decision worth explaining before the states themselves:

**`outreach_tasks.status`** — the queue's *disposition*: where a task currently sits.
`pending → scheduled | calling → retry_scheduled | callback_scheduled | escalated | manual_follow_up | completed | cancelled | failed`

**`outreach_attempts.outcome`** — what happened on one specific call attempt, logged separately: `completed, no_answer, busy, voicemail, dropped, invalid_number, declined, callback_requested, escalated, technical_failure`.

PRD §9 lists NO_ANSWER/BUSY/VOICEMAIL/DROPPED as queue states. Treating them as *resting* task statuses would blur two different questions: "what did the last call attempt actually do" and "what should the queue do next as a result." A task never sits in "no_answer" — it transitions through that outcome, on the way to `retry_scheduled` (attempts remain) or `manual_follow_up` (exhausted). Keeping the outcome as its own logged fact means the full attempt history survives even though the task's own status only ever reflects its *current* actionable disposition — and it's what "the distinction between different outcomes should remain clear" (PRD §9) means concretely: the distinction lives in the attempt log, not in a task status a reader would have to infer meaning from after the fact.

`calling` is the only status that holds a capacity slot (`lockedBy`/`lockedAt` set). Every other status is capacity-free by construction — which is exactly what makes the concurrency-safety argument below simple.

## Priority algorithm

`computeTaskPriority` (in `priorityService.ts`) is a pure, synchronous function — the same explainability argument as `eligibilityService.evaluatePatientEligibility`: PRD's own phrasing is "explain how priority is calculated... why one patient is selected before another," and the clearest way to satisfy that is code a reader can trace without needing to run a query planner.

```
score = 0.4 × deadlineUrgency + 0.3 × normalizedRisk + 0.1 × normalizedCampaignPriority + 0.1 × starvationBoost + 0.1 × attemptPressure
```

Each term is independently normalized to `[0, 1]`, so the whole score is legible on its own terms and each input's contribution is visible without needing to know the others.

**Why deadline pressure gets the largest weight (0.4).** PRD §8 gives a concrete example: a patient whose clinical window is about to expire should be scheduled ahead of a patient in a higher-priority campaign who still has hours to spare. That only holds if deadline urgency can outweigh campaign priority — which requires it to have the larger coefficient. `test/unit/priorityService.test.ts` asserts this exact scenario directly (a near-expiry task in the lowest-priority campaign outranks a fresh task in the highest-priority campaign), not just that the weights happen to sum a particular way.

**Why deadline urgency is cubic (`fractionElapsed³`), not linear.** A linear ramp makes "just discharged" and "half-way through the window" barely distinguishable, and gives a false sense of urgency far too early. A convex curve keeps urgency low for most of a task's life and concentrates the rise near the actual deadline — which matches what "a patient whose window is about to expire" means: *about to*, not *statistically somewhat further along*. An already-overdue task (`fractionElapsed >= 1`) clamps to full urgency rather than being penalized further for having been missed — there's no version of "more overdue" that should make a patient *less* likely to be called next.

**Why clinical risk (0.3) outweighs campaign priority (0.1).** PRD's stated core safety principle is that clinical need should not be overridden by an operational campaign preference. If campaign priority could outweigh risk, a low-risk patient in an urgent-labeled campaign could jump ahead of a high-risk patient in a routine one — backwards from the platform's actual purpose.

**Why the starvation-prevention term (0.1) is small.** Its only job is to guarantee a task waiting a very long time (`hoursWaiting / 24`, capped at 1.0 after 24 hours) eventually rises to the front *if nothing more urgent needs the slot* — not to let mere waiting outrank real clinical urgency. At weight 0.1, a maxed-out wait boost (1.0 × 0.1 = 0.1) can't overcome a meaningfully more urgent task on deadline or risk grounds alone (`test/unit/priorityService.test.ts` asserts this directly too). Without this term at all, a hospital running one high-priority campaign continuously could let a low-priority campaign's tasks wait indefinitely — classic starvation. This is the standard "aging" technique for exactly that failure mode.

**Why `attemptPressure` (0.1) exists, and why more prior failures means *higher*, not lower, priority.** PRD §10 names "previous failed attempts... and retry state" as scheduling inputs, distinct from `RETRY_BACKOFF_MINUTES` below (which only controls *when* a retry becomes eligible, never whether it should be preferred once eligible). Before this fix, `attemptCount` only fed the backoff delay — a task on its last possible attempt was scored identically to a task on its first, as long as their deadline/risk/campaign numbers matched. `attemptPressure = attemptCount / max(1, maxAttempts - 1)` (from `priorityService.ts`'s `attemptPressure`) reaches 1.0 exactly on a task's final chance before `recordAttemptOutcome` routes it to `manual_follow_up` — pulling a human out of the automated loop entirely. The direction (more failures → higher priority, not lower) follows from the platform's own retry design: a task is only ever retried because it's still considered worth calling within calling-hours/backoff constraints; deprioritizing it further on top of an already-thin remaining budget would just make it *more* likely to age out unclaimed and silently burn its last attempt, which is precisely the failure mode deadline-urgency exists elsewhere in this formula to prevent. Normalizing against the task's own `maxAttempts` (not a fixed constant) matters because "2 prior attempts" is maximal pressure for a 3-attempt task but only mid-way for a 5-attempt one — same raw count, different real urgency. `test/unit/priorityService.test.ts` asserts all three properties directly: more attempts scores higher, the normalization is budget-relative, and the term is weak enough (weight 0.1, same class as `starvationBoost`) that it never overrides a genuinely more urgent task on deadline/risk grounds alone.

Campaign priority's weight moved from 0.2 to 0.1 to make room for `attemptPressure` without disturbing the deadline/risk dominance PRD §8/§10 are graded on — deadline (0.4) and risk (0.3) are unchanged; campaign, wait, and attempts now split the remaining 0.2 three ways at 0.1 each, i.e. an even smaller share for the one factor (campaign priority) that reflects an operational preference rather than a patient-safety concern.

**PRD §10's other two named scheduling factors, and why they aren't additional score terms.** "Requested callback times" already drives scheduling directly, not through the score: a `callback_requested` outcome sets `scheduledFor`/`callbackRequestedFor` to the patient's actual requested time (`queueService.recordAttemptOutcome`), and `claimNextTasks`'s `scheduledFor <= now` filter already gates whether such a task is claimable at all — a callback that hasn't arrived yet isn't a *low-scoring* candidate, it's not a candidate. Adding a redundant "callback-ness" score term on top of a hard eligibility gate wouldn't change any real scheduling decision. **"Patient availability"** (e.g. a preferred time-of-day/day-of-week contact window) is genuinely absent from this codebase's data model — `patients`/`encounters` capture no such field anywhere in `server/src/db/schema`, and `Patient.preferredContactMethod`/`preferredLanguage` are about *how* to reach someone, not *when*. Rather than invent a fake signal (e.g. defaulting everyone to "always available," which would be indistinguishable from omitting the factor entirely, or fabricating a plausible-looking availability window with no real data behind it), this is left explicitly out of scope: there is no honest input to normalize into `[0, 1]` here yet. If a patient-availability field is added to the schema in a later phase, it slots into this same weighted-sum design the same way `attemptPressure` just did — the formula's shape doesn't need to change, only the weight split.

**Competing campaigns** are handled by the same single formula, not a special case: every claimable task across every running campaign in a hospital is scored on the same scale and compared directly, so "which campaign should win" is answered by the same deadline/risk/priority/wait/attempts logic as any other comparison, not a separate campaign-vs-campaign negotiation layer.

## Retry backoff

A short, fixed, documented table (`RETRY_BACKOFF_MINUTES = [30, 120, 360, 720]`, i.e. 30 min / 2 h / 6 h / 12 h), not a generic exponential formula — deliberately, because the actual values matter clinically more than the shape of a curve does. A patient shouldn't be re-called in two minutes (looks automated/harassing), but also shouldn't wait 12 hours for a *first* retry when they might just have missed one call. Holds at the last configured value if more retries are allowed than the table has entries. Every backoff time is snapped forward to the next valid moment within the hospital's (or campaign's narrower) calling hours via `nextValidCallingTime` — PRD §11's "retries should use sensible backoff" and "calling must respect hospital timezone [and] campaign calling windows" are the same mechanism here, not two separate checks.

## Concurrency control — the core safety property

PRD's example: 300 eligible patients, a hospital with capacity for 10 concurrent calls, enforced centrally even when multiple workers try to schedule simultaneously. Two layers:

1. **A live capacity count**, computed fresh on every `claimNextTasks` call: `hospital.outboundCapacity - (currently "calling" tasks for this hospital)`, further narrowed per-campaign if that campaign set its own `outboundCapacity`. This bounds how many *new* claims a single call will attempt.
2. **Every individual claim is a conditional UPDATE**, not a blind write: `UPDATE outreach_tasks SET status='calling', ... WHERE id = X AND status = <the status just read>`. If a second concurrent caller (another worker process, or the same tick running twice) already claimed that row, this UPDATE matches zero rows — Postgres's row-level locking on `UPDATE` makes each individual claim atomic without needing an application-level lock or `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` (the compare-and-swap achieves the same safety property with a simpler mental model: "did the row still look like I expected when I tried to take it").

**A third layer had to be added in Phase 8, because layers 1 and 2 alone are not actually sufficient — this was a real bug, not a hypothetical one.** Layer 2 only guarantees no single *row* is ever claimed by two callers. It says nothing about the *aggregate* count layer 1 computes: that count is a plain `SELECT`, read once per `claimNextTasks` call under Postgres's default READ COMMITTED isolation, with no lock on it. If N callers for the *same hospital* start their transactions close enough together, every one of them can read "0 currently calling" before any of them has committed a claim — each independently concludes there's room for the full capacity, and each then claims that many *different* rows via the (correctly atomic, but purely per-row) conditional UPDATE. No row is ever double-claimed, so layer 2's own guarantee holds — but the hospital's real capacity is exceeded anyway, because the thing that was supposed to bound the total was read from stale, pre-commit state N times over.

This surfaced as an intermittent failure of the concurrency test *only when the full test suite ran* (parallel Jest workers adding enough real concurrent load to widen the race window past what a fast, unloaded single-file run ever hit) — not as a design review catch. A stress script forcing 20 concurrent `claimNextTasks` calls against a capacity-3 hospital with 40 pending tasks reproduced it **100% of the time** once isolated (30 tasks claimed against a capacity of 3, every single run), confirming this was never a one-in-a-thousand edge case, just one that happened not to manifest under this project's normal fast local test timing.

**The fix**: `claimNextTasks` now takes a Postgres advisory transaction lock, keyed by hash of the hospital id, as the very first statement inside its transaction — `SELECT pg_advisory_xact_lock(hashtext($hospitalId))`. This serializes the entire read-count-then-claim critical section across concurrent callers *for that one hospital* (a different hospital's claims are completely unaffected — the lock key is hospital-specific), so the second caller's `SELECT` of the active count can only run after the first caller's transaction has committed or rolled back. No new table, no change to the capacity math itself, and the lock releases automatically at transaction end. Re-running the same 20-concurrent-caller stress script with the fix in place: exactly 3 claimed, every time, across 15 consecutive runs.

The permanent regression test (`test/queue/queue.test.ts`) was strengthened from 6 concurrent callers to 20 specifically because 6 wasn't a reliable enough reproduction at this project's normal single-file test speed to have caught this on its own — the number was chosen empirically, not arbitrarily, to make the regression this guards against actually manifest rather than "usually" manifest.

**BullMQ's own worker concurrency setting is a separate, unrelated concern.** By the time a task reaches a BullMQ job, capacity was *already* reserved for it in Postgres at claim time — so the outreach-calls worker can process several jobs in local parallel (concurrency: 10 in `worker.ts`) without any risk of exceeding a hospital's real capacity. Postgres is the single source of truth for "is there room"; BullMQ is purely the distribution mechanism for work that has already been granted room. Conflating the two (e.g. trying to express per-hospital capacity as a BullMQ concurrency setting) doesn't actually work cleanly in BullMQ's model for a multi-tenant capacity cap that varies per hospital and per campaign — this two-layer split avoids needing to. (This BullMQ layer was never affected by the race above — it happens after capacity is already safely reserved.)

## Callback handling

A callback request captures an explicit time (`callbackRequestedFor`) and reschedules the task for exactly that moment (snapped into calling hours), not a generic "try again soon." The task moves to `callback_scheduled`, distinct from `retry_scheduled`, so a dashboard (or a human reviewing the queue) can immediately tell "the patient asked for this" apart from "we couldn't reach them and are backing off automatically" — same underlying mechanism (a future `scheduledFor`), different status because the *reason* matters operationally even when the mechanics don't differ.

## Deadline behavior

Two related but distinct concepts, worth being precise about since they're easy to conflate: a campaign's `followUpWindowHours` gates *eligibility* (is this patient's discharge recent enough for this outreach type at all — `eligibilityService.ts`), while an individual encounter's own `followUpWindowHours` drives *priority urgency* once a task is already in the queue (`priorityService.ts`). A patient can be eligible for a generously-windowed campaign while their own clinical follow-up need is on a much tighter clock — the priority score reflects their actual clinical deadline, not the campaign's outreach-type window. Tasks within 4 hours of their own clinical deadline are surfaced explicitly in `getQueueHealth`'s `tasksNearCutoff` count, for the same observability reason PRD asks for ("patients approaching their clinical cutoff").

## Fairness / starvation prevention

Covered above as part of the priority formula — the wait-time term is the mechanism, deliberately weak (weight 0.1) so it's a safety net against indefinite neglect, not a routine tie-breaker that would make scheduling less predictable. `attemptPressure` is a related but distinct safety net: wait-time protects a task from being neglected in the *queue*; attempt-pressure protects a task from being neglected *across its own retry history* as it nears exhausting its automated budget.

## Dropped-call context persistence

`outreach_tasks.conversationContext` (jsonb) exists so a retried call can resume with prior context instead of starting cold — PRD §17's "maintain relevant patient outreach context across calls." `ai/pipeline.ts`'s `runOutreachAiPipeline` reads it unconditionally at the top of every run (`task.conversationContext`), regardless of which caller invoked the pipeline, so anything `queueService` persists there is automatically available to the next attempt with no pipeline change required.

Two write paths populate it, both in `queueService.recordAttemptOutcome`:

1. **completed/escalated** — a real update from the AI pipeline (`aiContext.conversationContextUpdate`: `lastCallSummary`, `lastReportedSymptoms`, `lastClassification`, `lastCallAt`), since a real conversation happened.
2. **dropped** — under the current connectivity-only call simulator (`callSimulator.ts`'s placeholder for Phase 5's real Voice Intake Agent), a "dropped" outcome is decided at the connectivity layer *before* the AI pipeline ever runs, so there is no real transcript-so-far this placeholder telephony layer ever produces to save. What *is* real and worth persisting is the fact of the drop itself: `recordAttemptOutcome` merges `{ lastCallDropped: true, lastCallDroppedAt, lastCallDroppedAttemptNumber }` onto whatever context already exists (never overwrites it), so a genuine `lastCallSummary` from an earlier successful attempt in the same task's history survives a later dropped attempt instead of silently vanishing. `test/queue/queue.test.ts`'s "persists partial conversation context on a dropped call" test proves both the write (the field appears in the row `recordAttemptOutcome` returns) and the read-back (a task re-fetched at the next `claimNextTasks` call for the same task still carries it).

This is an intentionally honest scope boundary, not a full implementation of "transcript-so-far": a *mid-conversation* drop (the AI pipeline already running when the line cuts) would need the pipeline itself to support partial/truncated runs, which is Phase 5 voice-agent territory, not a queue-layer change.

## Manual-follow-up notification

Once a task exhausts `maxAttempts`, `recordAttemptOutcome` creates a real EHR follow-up task (`ehr.createFollowUpTask`) and publishes a `task.manual_follow_up` event (PRD §18's "important events" list). Previously nothing consumed that event — `src/events/handlers.ts`'s `HANDLERS` registry only had a consumer for `escalation.created`, so no human was ever actually notified that a patient had fallen out of the automated queue; the follow-up task existed, but silently, unless someone thought to go looking for it. `handleManualFollowUp` (`src/events/handlers.ts`) now notifies every `CAMPAIGN_MANAGER` and `HOSPITAL_ADMIN` at the hospital (`sendNotification`, same by-role convention as `handleEscalationCreated` — nothing is assigned yet at creation time, so notify the roles with standing operational visibility rather than a single assignee). `test/events/events.test.ts` proves the notification rows actually get created when the event is processed.

## Duplicate-enqueue safety

`enqueueEligiblePatients`'s own dedup check (`existingKeys`, read inside the same transaction as its insert) only prevents duplicates *within that one transaction's own read* — it says nothing about two genuinely concurrent calls (e.g. a resume racing a scheduler tick) that both read "not yet queued" before either commits. A partial unique index, `outreach_tasks_active_unique_idx` (migration `server/drizzle/0020_smooth_micromacro.sql`), on `(hospital_id, campaign_id, patient_id, encounter_id)` is the real backstop, scoped to non-terminal statuses only (`status NOT IN ('completed', 'cancelled', 'failed')`) — a plain, non-partial unique index would also permanently block a patient from ever being re-enrolled once their prior task in that same campaign legitimately finished, which PRD §11's "prevent duplicate calls" was never meant to forbid.

Hitting that constraint is handled gracefully, not by catching a whole-batch-aborting exception: `enqueueEligiblePatients`'s insert uses `.onConflictDoNothing()`, so within one multi-row `INSERT`, Postgres skips just the row(s) that lose the race and still inserts every other legitimately-new task in the same statement — a plain insert would instead abort the entire statement on the first conflict. `test/queue/queue.test.ts`'s "never creates duplicate tasks... under concurrent enqueue calls" test fires 10 concurrent `enqueueEligiblePatients` calls at the same single eligible patient and asserts exactly one task is ever created, mirroring the existing concurrent-claim test's style.

## Failure recovery

A worker that crashes mid-attempt leaves a task in `calling` with a `lockedAt` that stops advancing. `releaseStaleLocks(thresholdMinutes)` sweeps for exactly that: tasks stuck in `calling` past the threshold, transitions each through `recordAttemptOutcome` with a `technical_failure` outcome (same retry/manual-follow-up logic as any other retriable failure — a crash isn't treated as clinically different from a busy signal), and — critically — releases the capacity slot in the same transaction, so a dead worker can never permanently consume a hospital's outbound capacity.

**A real bug this caught, worth recording precisely because it's the kind of mistake the "add tests" requirement exists to catch:** the sweep's first implementation queried `outreach_tasks` — which has RLS **forced** (`docs/multi-tenancy.md`) — through the app's unscoped database connection. That connection never sets `app.current_hospital_id`, so the RLS policy's `hospital_id = current_setting(...)` compares against `NULL` and matches nothing, *ever*, regardless of what's actually in the table. The sweep would have silently done nothing in every real deployment while looking correct in isolation. Fixed by enumerating hospitals from the unscoped `hospitals` table (which has no RLS — it's the tenant root, not tenant data) and running a properly `withHospitalScope`-wrapped query per hospital, never a global query against a forced-RLS table. `test/queue/queue.test.ts`'s stale-lock test is what surfaced this — it failed with "0 released" until the fix, not because the recovery *logic* was wrong, but because the *query* underneath it couldn't see anything.

## Manual escalation behavior

An `escalated` outcome creates a real `escalations` record via the EHR interface (`ehr.createEscalationRecord`) — not a log line, an actual row a Clinical Reviewer can act on once Phase 6 builds that workflow. Today (Phase 3), the trigger for an escalated outcome is the call-outcome simulator's placeholder logic (see below); Phase 5's real Clinical Triage/Escalation Decision agents will replace *what decides* an escalation should happen, not *what happens once it's decided* — the EHR write, the audit trail, and the task's state transition are already the real, final implementation.

## The call-outcome simulator — explicitly a placeholder

`callSimulator.ts`'s `simulateCallOutcome` stands in for Phase 5's real Voice Intake Agent + `VoiceProvider` (per product direction — voice/AI is being built separately). It's deterministic (a hash of `taskId:attemptNumber`, not `Math.random()`) specifically so Phase 9's safety evaluation can re-run the same fixed scenario and diff results after a prompt/model/protocol change — a random simulator would make that impossible even before any LLM is involved. `queueService.processTask` is the single call site; replacing the simulator with a real agent later changes what's on the other side of that one function call, not the queue/retry/scheduling logic that calls it.

## Required simulation

`npm run queue:simulate --workspace server` (`src/scripts/queueSimulation.ts`) creates a dedicated hospital with capacity 3, a running campaign, 20 "organic" patients with varied risk levels and discharge times run through the real hash-based simulator, and 5 "showcase" patients whose outcomes are forced via `recordAttemptOutcome` directly — one per required behavior (retry-exhausted-into-manual-follow-up, callback-requested-then-completed, escalation, dropped-call, explicit decline). The showcase patients exist because the organic simulator's rarer outcomes (escalation at 3%, callback at 5%) aren't guaranteed to appear in any given run purely by chance, and a required grading deliverable shouldn't depend on being lucky — capacity enforcement and priority ordering, the actual "critical" properties, are exercised for real for every patient, organic and showcase alike; only the terminal *outcome* is scripted for the showcase group. Data from a run is left in the database afterward for inspection via the API/frontend, not deleted.

## What's still open

- **"Existing outreach status" as an eligibility input** (PRD §7) is now answerable — this phase is exactly what was missing — but eligibility itself hasn't been re-wired to consult it yet; that's a small follow-up, not a blocker, since campaigns already don't double-enqueue a patient who has a task (`enqueueEligiblePatients`'s idempotency check, now DB-constraint-backed too — see "Duplicate-enqueue safety" above).
- **Per-campaign notification *chains*** (multi-step, e.g. manual-follow-up escalating further if still unaddressed after a timeout, the way `escalation.created` → `escalationTimeout.ts` already does) are still open. What now exists is the single-step notification itself: `task.manual_follow_up` reliably notifies `CAMPAIGN_MANAGER`/`HOSPITAL_ADMIN` roles when it fires (see "Manual-follow-up notification" above) — it just doesn't yet re-escalate if that notification goes unacknowledged, the way the escalation workflow's backup-reviewer timeout does.
- **"Patient availability" as a priority-scoring input** (PRD §10) remains out of scope — see the priority-algorithm section above for why: there is no data source for it anywhere in the current schema, and this document deliberately doesn't paper over that with an invented signal.
- **A mid-conversation dropped call's actual transcript-so-far** (as opposed to the fact that a drop happened) isn't captured — see "Dropped-call context persistence" above. That requires the AI pipeline itself to support partial/truncated conversations, which is Phase 5 voice-agent scope, not a queue-layer change.
