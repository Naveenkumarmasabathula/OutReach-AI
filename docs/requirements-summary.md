# Requirements Summary — Must-Have Features

Source: `Project_Requirements (1).pdf` v2.0, "Multi-Hospital Post-Discharge Outreach Platform." This is the must-have feature set from §31 ("Prototype Scope and Prioritization"), cross-referenced with the detailed sections (§3–§30) that define each item. Should-have and nice-to-have items are intentionally excluded here — see §31 of the PDF for those; they are out of scope until the must-haves are solid.

The PRD's own grading priority table (§34):

| Priority | Evaluation Area |
|---|---|
| 1 — Critical | Queue design, concurrency, retry logic, multi-tenancy, AI triage, escalation consensus, safety evaluation |
| 2 — Important | Campaign management, dashboards, mock EHR, workflows, observability, reliability |
| 3 — Enhancement | Real telephony, advanced analytics, richer UI, additional integrations |

## 1. Multi-tenancy & Access Control
Backend-enforced tenant isolation across APIs, DB, workers, AI retrieval, tools, background jobs, analytics. Four roles: Platform Admin, Hospital Admin, Campaign Manager, Clinical Reviewer. Auth, authorization, and RBAC are required parts of the system, not optional UI features.

## 2. Hospital Onboarding & Configuration
Platform admins create/configure hospitals: identity, contact info, timezone, permitted calling hours, outreach protocols, escalation contacts, outbound calling capacity, retry rules, notification preferences, knowledge resources, mock EHR settings. Lifecycle: created → configured → admins assigned → protocols/rules provided → ready for campaigns. Calling windows and clinical follow-up windows must be configurable per campaign/hospital.

## 3. Patient & Discharge Data
Simulated discharge feed, ~200–500 patients across multiple hospitals, with enough variation to demonstrate queue prioritization, clinical risk, different discharge times, follow-up requirements, successful contacts, failed attempts, callbacks, and escalations. Structured healthcare data model (simplified FHIR-like, not exact FHIR compliance): Patient, Encounter, Observation, Condition, Medication, CarePlan, Procedure, Organization, Communication, Task.

## 4. Mock EHR Abstraction Layer
A real EHR is not required; a mock EHR is expected, but it must be a genuine abstraction layer. Read: patient, encounter, discharge, condition, observation, care-plan. Write: communication records, structured observations, follow-up tasks, escalation records, mock encounter updates. AI must never directly manipulate database tables — it requests a structured operation through a controlled tool, and the backend performs authorization, schema validation, business-rule validation, and the actual operation. The interface should be replaceable with a real EHR integration later; failures must be represented explicitly.

## 5. Clinical Protocols & Knowledge Retrieval
Each hospital maintains hospital-specific post-discharge protocols and knowledge (follow-up questions, red-flag indicators, specialty instructions, approved patient guidance, contact windows, escalation contacts, operational rules). Retrieval must always be tenant-aware — a Hospital A patient must never cause retrieval of Hospital B's content. Clinical guidance must preserve its source/protocol reference for traceability. Retrieval should be task-specific, not a dump of the patient's entire history or every hospital document.

## 6. Campaign Management
A campaign groups patients requiring a particular type of post-discharge outreach. Config: hospital, name/description, eligibility criteria, clinical follow-up window, calling hours, priority rules, retry limits, campaign dates, outbound capacity, escalation configuration. Lifecycle: `DRAFT → READY → SCHEDULED → RUNNING → PAUSED → COMPLETED` (with optional `CANCELLED`/`FAILED` terminal states). A workload estimate (eligible patients, expected attempts) must be shown before activation. Pausing stops new outbound work while letting active calls finish safely; resuming recalculates eligible work rather than blindly restarting every previous task. Campaign managers can start, pause, resume, cancel, and reprioritize.

## 7. Patient Eligibility Engine
Considers hospital, discharge status/date, campaign rules, clinical follow-up window, communication eligibility/consent assumptions, patient-specific requirements, and existing outreach status. Must be evaluated consistently and explainably. Operational errors must not silently remove patients from outreach — failures must be exposed with a recovery path.

## 8. Outbound Queue & Scheduling — CRITICAL
One of the most important grading areas. The platform must centrally enforce outbound capacity limits (e.g., 300 eligible patients, 10 concurrent calls) even when multiple workers attempt to schedule calls simultaneously. The queue must optimize successful outreach within each patient's clinical window rather than processing in insertion order, considering: clinical risk, time since discharge, time remaining before the follow-up deadline, patient availability, previous failed attempts, requested callback times, campaign priority, and retry state. An actual prioritization algorithm must be designed and documented — how priority is calculated, how competing campaigns are handled, how starvation is prevented, why one patient is selected before another. The scheduler must account for deadline pressure (a high-priority-campaign patient with many hours remaining may reasonably be scheduled after a patient whose clinical window is about to expire). Capacity reservation must be safely coordinated — two workers must never reserve the same slot or exceed the configured concurrency limit.

## 9. Queue States, Retries, Callbacks, Failure Handling
Explicit outreach-task state model, e.g.: `PENDING, SCHEDULED, CALLING, CONNECTED, COMPLETED, NO_ANSWER, BUSY, VOICEMAIL, DROPPED, RETRY_SCHEDULED, CALLBACK_SCHEDULED, ESCALATED, MANUAL_FOLLOW_UP, FAILED`. Distinct handling for busy line, voicemail, no answer, invalid number, rejected call, network failure, dropped call, success, and technical failure, since these influence future scheduling. Retry behavior considers attempt count, clinical window, calling hours, patient preference, campaign priority, and previous attempt timing, using sensible backoff (not immediate re-calling). A dropped call must persist its partial state — the next attempt should retain relevant context rather than restarting from zero. A callback request must capture and schedule the requested time explicitly, not just return the patient to the generic queue. After max retries, the system creates a visible manual follow-up task and notifies appropriate staff. Calling must respect hospital timezone, campaign calling windows, and patient communication preferences. Near the end of a clinical window, the scheduler should prioritize patients at risk of missing the deadline. Workers must recover from crashes: detect stale tasks holding capacity, release that capacity, and either retry or move the task to an explicit manual/failed state. Duplicate calls and duplicate side effects must be prevented.

## 10. Required Queue Simulation
Mandatory: a simulation with ~20–30 mock patients, mixed clinical risk, different deadlines, limited calling capacity, successful calls, no-answers, busy outcomes, dropped calls, retries, callbacks, and escalations — evaluators must be able to observe the queue changing dynamically and verify concurrency limits are respected. Requires a short **queue design document** explaining and justifying (not just describing) the state machine, priority calculation, retry/backoff strategy, concurrency control, callback handling, deadline behavior, fairness/starvation prevention, failure recovery, and manual escalation behavior.

## 11. AI Agent Architecture
Do not implement the whole product as one unrestricted AI agent. Must separate responsibilities into at least: **Voice Intake Agent** (manages the conversation, protocol-driven questions, captures responses, identifies symptoms, handles clarification, decides when to end/transfer), **Clinical Triage Agent** (converts conversation into structured observations and compares against approved hospital protocols), **Escalation Decision System** (combines independent assessments to decide whether human attention is required), **Documentation Agent** (converts the interaction into a structured record: call summary, patient-reported symptoms, observations, outcome, triage result, escalation status, follow-up requirements). Exact model providers/agent framework intentionally left open.

## 12. Voice Intake and Conversation
Should behave like a structured healthcare outreach conversation, not an unrestricted chatbot — uses patient/campaign context, follows the hospital-approved protocol, asks necessary questions, identifies relevant symptoms, handles reasonable conversational variation, avoids unsupported medical claims. Real outbound calling is a strong enhancement (should-have); a deterministic call simulator is acceptable for demonstrating the core queue + AI workflow (must-have). Every call maintains a record: patient, campaign, timestamps, duration, outcome, conversation record/transcript where available, AI outputs, triage result, escalation result, documentation status.

## 13. Clinical Triage — Structured AI Output
Any AI output that application logic depends on must be structured and backend-validatable. A triage result contains a classification (`routine, concerning, urgent, or uncertain`), observed indicators, evidence from the conversation, relevant protocol references, confidence/uncertainty information, and an escalation recommendation. Exact schema is open but must be explicit and machine-readable. The backend must validate AI output before use; malformed output should trigger a controlled repair or retry, and repeated failure must become an explicit operational failure rather than being silently accepted. AI output must be traceable to the underlying conversation and retrieved protocol evidence.

## 14. Escalation Consensus & Clinical Safety — CRITICAL
The most safety-sensitive part of the system. Must not rely entirely on a single model response to decide whether a patient needs human attention — use multiple independent assessments, agents, models, or equivalent reasoning paths and compare results. Consensus mechanism is open (majority voting, weighted voting, an arbiter, rule-assisted consensus, or another defensible approach) — what matters is that disagreement is detected and handled, not hidden. Example: if one assessment classifies a patient as routine while another identifies a protocol red flag, the system must recognize the disagreement and apply a conservative escalation strategy. Must record individual assessments, disagreement, consensus result, evidence used, and the final escalation decision. Potential escalation triggers: emergency symptoms, deterioration, severe/worsening symptoms, protocol-defined red flags, concerning patient statements, or significant uncertainty. The AI must not diagnose the patient, prescribe medication, change treatment, or provide unsupported clinical judgment. Patient-provided content, retrieved documents, and model-generated text must never be able to override system policies, authorization boundaries, or clinical safety rules — treat these as separate trust domains (prompt-injection defense).

## 15. Safety Evaluation & False-Negative Measurement — CRITICAL
Create a fixed safety evaluation dataset containing known routine, concerning, urgent, ambiguous, incomplete-information, conflicting-information, and adversarial cases. Run the triage/escalation system against this dataset and compare expected vs. actual outcomes. Must report, at minimum, true positives, false positives, true negatives, false negatives, and the **false-negative rate** — false negatives (failing to escalate a high-risk case) are called out as more dangerous than an unnecessary escalation. The evaluation must be repeatable after changes to models, prompts, retrieval, protocols, or consensus logic, and must be reproducible/auditable rather than a collection of manually selected examples. The report should include representative disagreement cases, limitations, observed weaknesses, and improvements.

## 16. Controlled AI Tools & Persistent Context
AI agents must interact with the application only through controlled tools — never unrestricted database access. Possible tools: patient lookup, encounter lookup, protocol search, observation lookup, campaign status lookup, callback scheduling, call outcome recording, escalation creation, notification requests, communication recording, mock EHR updates. General pattern: **AI request → authorization → schema validation → business rules → execution → audit → result**. The platform must also maintain relevant patient outreach context across calls (previous outcomes, callback preferences, symptoms already discussed, unanswered questions, escalation state, outstanding follow-up requirements), retrieved task-specifically rather than blindly loading full patient history into every model call.

## 17. Documentation and Call Records
Every outreach attempt produces a structured record identifying the patient, hospital, campaign, call, timestamps, outcome, conversation information, AI assessments, triage result, escalation decision, documentation status, and relevant follow-up actions. Supported outcomes: successful completion, no answer, busy, voicemail, dropped call, invalid number, declined interaction, callback request, escalation, technical failure, manual follow-up. Documentation must be traceable back to the conversation, and where applicable sent to the mock EHR.

## 18. Workflows, Events and Notifications
Use asynchronous events and background processing for discharge ingestion, campaign preparation, queue scheduling, calls, retries, EHR sync, notifications, analytics, and evaluation. Important events: patient import, campaign creation/start/pause, call scheduling/start/completion/failure, retry scheduling, callback requests, escalation creation/acknowledgement/resolution, EHR updates, notification delivery. Events may trigger multi-step workflows (e.g., an escalation notifies a nurse, then escalates to a backup reviewer if not acknowledged within a configured period; a failed call may trigger retry eligibility and return the task to scheduling). The workflow system must tolerate duplicate events, retries, failed consumers, and out-of-order processing — important operations must be idempotent. Where practical, non-engineering users should be able to configure operational behavior (escalation notifications, reviewer timeouts, callback reminders, campaign pauses, retry notifications) without changing AI logic.

## 19. Escalation Management & Human-in-the-Loop
Escalations are first-class operational records containing patient, hospital, campaign, call, trigger, clinical indicators, triage result, consensus result, priority, creation time, assigned reviewer, status, resolution, resolution timestamp. Lifecycle: `OPEN → ASSIGNED → IN_REVIEW → WAITING_FOR_INFORMATION → RESOLVED` (with optional `CLOSED`). Authorized staff must be able to inspect patient context, conversation, AI outputs, protocol evidence, and escalation rationale before resolving the case, and to acknowledge, assign, reassign, resolve, and record follow-up actions. Notifications may use an internal dashboard, email, SMS, or another configurable mechanism, and delivery must be observable. Human actions, operational overrides, and clinical resolutions must be auditable.

## 20. Dashboards and Analytics
Operational dashboards, not just technical monitoring. **Campaign Manager dashboard**: campaign progress, queue state, pending/active work, retries, completed/failed tasks, escalations, manual follow-ups, active call capacity, queue depth, oldest pending task, patients approaching clinical cutoff, plus permitted controls (start/pause/resume/reprioritize). **Hospital Admin dashboard**: hospital-wide visibility into campaigns, outreach volume, contact rates, escalations, manual follow-ups, staff activity, EHR sync, protocols, system health. **Platform Admin dashboard**: authorized aggregate visibility across hospitals — activity, campaign volume, queue health, AI usage, errors, escalation statistics, system performance — but aggregate analytics must never bypass patient-level access controls. Authorized users also get a patient operational view (discharge info, outreach status, call history, outcomes, escalations, observations, documentation, follow-up actions). Analytics cover eligible patients, attempted/completed outreach, contact outcomes, average attempts, time-to-contact, queue wait, retry behavior, capacity utilization, escalations, manual follow-ups.

## 21. Observability, Audit and System Health
The system must make its behavior observable. Application monitoring: API latency/errors, authentication failures, background job status, queue depth, scheduling latency, concurrency utilization, retry backlog, stuck tasks, calling/notification/EHR failures. AI observability: agent/model used, latency, retrieval sources, structured-output validation, tool usage, disagreement, escalation evidence, token usage, estimated cost, failures where practical. Sensitive healthcare information must not be unnecessarily written into logs. Important operations must produce audit records (campaign create/pause, AI escalation assessments, consensus decisions, staff reviews, EHR updates, operational overrides) that are not casually editable or deletable. Health states: `Healthy, Degraded, Unavailable`, plus queue-specific health (active calls, capacity, pending work, oldest task, cutoff-risk tasks, failed tasks, stuck workers). Correlation/operation identifiers should be used for tracing asynchronous workflows.

## 22. Reliability, Idempotency and Recovery
Must explicitly handle failure rather than assume every external service succeeds: AI provider outages, voice provider failures, database failures, EHR failures, queue worker crashes, notification failures, malformed AI responses, timeouts, rate limits, interrupted calls. Use appropriate timeouts, retries, backoff, circuit breaking/fallback mechanisms, explicit failure states, and manual recovery paths. Operations where duplicate execution could cause harm (starting a call, recording an outcome, creating an escalation, sending a notification, writing to the EHR, scheduling a retry, processing a discharge, consuming an event) must be idempotent. Stuck tasks must be recoverable via heartbeat/timeout mechanisms; a failed worker must not permanently consume outbound capacity. Must prevent duplicate calls, duplicate escalations, and duplicate external side effects.

## 23. Security, Privacy and Clinical Guardrails
Required controls: authentication, authorization, RBAC, tenant isolation, secure APIs, input validation, secret management, audit logging, encryption where appropriate, data minimization, protection against sensitive information appearing unnecessarily in logs. Tenant context must be enforced consistently across APIs, databases, background workers, AI retrieval, tools, EHR integrations, and analytics. AI-specific security must include protection against prompt injection and malicious patient/document content — patient text and retrieved documents must never override system instructions, authorization rules, or clinical safety policies. The prototype must clearly distinguish prototype-level security from production compliance — do not claim HIPAA, SOC 2, or other certification unless actually satisfied; document what would need to change before real patient data could be used in production.

## 24. Architecture, APIs and Data Model
Technology stack/architecture intentionally open, but must clearly separate: frontend/authentication, tenant management, campaign management, eligibility, queue/scheduling, outbound calling, AI agents, protocol retrieval, healthcare data/EHR integration, background processing, workflows/notifications, analytics, observability. One clear system architecture diagram is sufficient (avoid multiple repetitive diagrams), supported by small state/sequence diagrams only where they materially improve understanding — the important architectural boundaries are AI↔controlled-tools, queue/calling infrastructure, the EHR abstraction, async events/workflows, and observability. The API should support authentication, hospitals/users, patients/encounters, campaigns, queue state, calls, escalations, protocols/knowledge, EHR operations, notifications, analytics, evaluation, and health. APIs need validation, authorization, consistent responses, pagination where appropriate, error handling, and separation between transport and business logic. The data model must capture relationships between hospitals, users, patients, encounters, discharges, campaigns, outreach tasks, calls, escalations, documentation, queue state, retries, events, audit records, and AI usage — designed with tenant isolation, indexing, history, operational state, and auditability in mind.

## 25. AI Model Abstraction and AI Usage
Avoid unnecessary coupling to a single AI provider. Where practical, define interfaces for conversational generation, structured generation, classification, retrieval, evaluation, speech processing, and document understanding. AI usage should be observable: model/provider, agent, request purpose, latency, success/failure, token usage, estimated cost where available. Prompts should have clear responsibilities, expected inputs/outputs, versioning, and evaluation coverage — at minimum distinguishing prompts for voice intake, clinical triage, escalation assessment, consensus, documentation, and evaluation. Must explain why particular models/AI approaches were selected and what tradeoffs were made.

## 26. Testing
Backend testing: authentication, authorization, tenant isolation, API validation, campaign lifecycle, eligibility. Queue testing: prioritization, concurrency, retries, backoff, deadlines, callbacks, worker failure, recovery, idempotency. AI testing: structured output validation, protocol grounding, rejection of unsupported clinical information, triage behavior, escalation behavior, disagreement handling. Safety testing: known escalation and non-escalation cases, must report false negatives. Background processing testing: retries, duplicate events, failed workers, recovery. EHR integration should be tested independently from the AI layer. The fixed safety evaluation dataset must be repeatable so model/prompt/retrieval/protocol changes can be compared against previous results.

## 27. Technology Selection and Deployment
Candidates may choose their own frontend, backend, database, queue, AI models, retrieval system, voice infrastructure, hosting platform, and supporting technologies — the important requirement is to justify choices based on reliability, development speed, scalability, maintainability, safety, and prototype constraints. Preferred outcome is a publicly accessible deployed prototype containing the frontend, backend, database, AI components, queue workers, dashboards, authentication, mock EHR, and background processing. Real telephony is encouraged but not mandatory. Secrets must be supplied through environment/deployment configuration and never committed to source control. If authentication is required for evaluation, provide safe demo credentials or another documented evaluation mechanism.

## 28. Required Deliverables (§33)
1. Deployed application URL + safe demo credentials/access instructions
2. Public GitHub repository (source, README, setup instructions, env config example, DB setup, mock EHR, queue simulation, tests, deployment instructions)
3. Demo video (onboarding → patient ingestion → campaign creation → queue behavior → constrained concurrency → retries → AI interaction → triage → escalation → human review → documentation → EHR update → analytics → system health)
4. Architecture documentation (one clear diagram + concise explanation of major services and boundaries)
5. Queue design document (states, scheduling logic, priority calculation, concurrency control, retry strategy, callback handling, clinical deadlines, fairness, failure recovery, manual follow-up)
6. Safety evaluation report (fixed dataset, expected vs. actual outcomes, FP/FN, false-negative rate, disagreement examples, limitations, improvements)
7. AI usage documentation (models, agents, retrieval approach, prompts, structured schemas, tools, consensus mechanism, evaluation process, failure handling)
8. Development AI usage documentation (prompts/workflows used during architecture, database, backend, frontend, queue, retry, voice, triage, consensus, EHR, testing, debugging, documentation work)
9. Known limitations and tradeoffs (what was simplified/omitted and why — evaluators must be able to distinguish intentional prototype tradeoffs from incomplete/broken functionality)

## End-to-End Success Scenario (§32)
Hospital created and configured → users assigned → discharge patients imported → campaign created → eligible patients identified → tasks enter the queue → limited calling capacity enforced → different call outcomes occur → retries and callbacks scheduled → AI conversation collects structured information → clinical triage evaluates the interaction → multiple assessments produce an escalation decision → an urgent case enters the human workflow → staff review the case → documentation is generated → the mock EHR is updated → dashboards reflect the resulting activity. The queue must demonstrably respect capacity, let higher-priority patients move ahead when justified, protect patients near their clinical cutoff, handle retries correctly, never drop dropped calls, honor callback requests, create manual follow-up after max retries, release capacity from failed workers, and prevent duplicate calls.
